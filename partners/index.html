The idea in one page

A proven center, brought to Bangkok in small, self-funding clusters.

We are not testing whether this works. We already know it does. The question this plan answers is how to copy Samui's success across Bangkok without betting the whole thing at once: small enough steps that each one pays for the next, sized so a single weak location never sinks the plan, and placed where the families already are, near schools and in affluent neighborhoods.

North star

Take what already works in Samui and make Rick Tew's NinjaGym the default kids' ninja gym across Bangkok and beyond, one small cluster at a time, so the network's own profit pays for its growth and the downside of any single site stays small.

The unit we build

Three centers make a cluster

A cluster is our unit for an investor who wants more than one center. Everything else in this plan is just this one box, repeated.

What a cluster is, and how an investor grows into one

One cluster = three centers. There is no separate "small" and "big" model: every center runs the same calculator, and the entry budget, rent, and revenue sliders set its size. The company co-invests ฿100K of each center's build, so an investor funds about ฿500K per center: a full cluster is roughly ฿1.5M of investor capital to open and, once mature, earns about ฿400K a month combined across all three (about ฿4.8M a year). These are cluster totals, not per-center; the goal network is ten of these clusters.

Start with one center, grow into a cluster. An investor can fund a single center to start (from about ฿250K, the 50% minimum, with the company co-investing ฿100K per center), then let their earnings accrue to open a second and third center, up to one full cluster. Growing beyond a single cluster needs a new agreement, so no small stake quietly rolls up many centers.

Or take the whole cluster up front, all three at once under one agreement.

Turn-key from day one: the NinjaGym app

A big reason a center can open and fill this fast is the NinjaGym app, which runs the whole operation out of the box: memberships and PINs, the POS and daily cash, check-ins, belt progress, loyalty, and birthdays. A new location is close to turn-key from the day it opens, and the system, not any one person, is what makes each center repeatable. The full NinjaGym proposal has the detail:

Where we build

Near schools and affluent neighborhoods

The whole model rests on each center finding its own families. Site selection is not an afterthought, it is the plan. Two simple rules place every center.

Near schools
Foot traffic
Lean centers near schools, where the after-school and weekend demand already exists. Lower rent and entry budget, quicker to fill, and a natural feeder for birthdays and holiday camps.
Affluent areas
Spend + space
Bigger-budget centers anchor affluent neighborhoods, with the space and family spending to support a full climb zone, game room, and events. Same model, larger entry slider.
Spacing matters
Own catchment
Centers are spread across Bangkok's districts, never stacked, so each one adds new families instead of splitting the same ones. Fresh catchment is what lets a center hit its number.
Why this de-risks it

Placing lean centers where children already gather (schools) and bigger builds where families can spend (affluent areas) means demand is found, not manufactured. It is the single biggest lever on whether a center hits its modest number, and it costs nothing but discipline in choosing sites.

Bangkok first, then the other hubs

This roadmap prices the Bangkok build. The same cluster model is open in Thailand's other family and tourist hubs for a partner who wants a region of their own. The structure, the flywheel, and the partnership terms are identical; only the market changes.

Bangkok this plan Phuket Chiang Mai Pattaya Hua Hin

Phuket, Chiang Mai, Pattaya, and Hua Hin are premium and tourist markets with higher rents and build costs, so a cluster in one of them is a larger investment than the Bangkok figures on this page. Bangkok is the focus of this roadmap; the other city clusters are available on request, priced to their market.

The people plan

Staffing: Bangkok's biggest hidden advantage

Every center is two-plus people, and finding the right people is the hardest thing about running Samui. The staffing case is one of the strongest and least obvious reasons Bangkok comes before Phuket or Pattaya: it is not just the biggest customer market in Thailand, it is by far the biggest hiring market for exactly the kind of person NinjaGym needs.

Bangkok moved to the ฿400/day tier on 1 July 2025 (so did Koh Samui and Phuket, so the legal floor is the same everywhere we would build; what differs is supply). The model's ฿20K default sits above minimum and near the Bangkok average, enough to hire well without inflating the cost base, with the wage slider there to stress-test higher. English-strong Thai staff price above average; the budget leaves room for one English-strong lead per center with juniors around them.

Three legal hiring pools beyond the obvious one

Pool 1 · The kick-start hire (15+)
Junior ninjas on staff
Thai law allows employment from age 15, with young-worker protections that happen to fit a kids' gym perfectly: no overtime, no work between 22:00 and 06:00, non-hazardous work only. A daytime kids' sports center is exactly that. Hiring 15 to 18 year olds as junior coaches gives local teens a first job with a real skill ladder, costs less than adult hires, and the ones who grew up training are often the best floor staff a kids' gym can get.
Pool 2 · Sport-science students
700,000+ students in town
Bangkok has roughly 40 universities with 700K+ enrolled students, including sport-science, physical-education, and coaching programs. Part-time and internship hires from these programs arrive already wanting the work NinjaGym offers, and the schedule (after-class evenings, weekends, holidays) matches exactly when the gym is busiest. Samui has no university at all; this pool simply does not exist there.
Pool 3 · Myanmar workers, legally
The MOU route
Myanmar nationals work legally in Thailand's service sector through the MOU system, with a named employer and a proper work permit, and many bring strong English. They widen the pool at competitive wages. The rule is strict compliance: MOU paperwork done properly per hire, no shortcuts, which is also what keeps an investor's center clean.

Hire the ones already training

The mission-fit rule, learned the hard way in Samui

A hire who only wants a paycheck does the minimum and quits; Rick's system needs people who actually care about kids, martial arts, sports, or mentoring. In Samui that person is rare and we make it work anyway. Bangkok is full of them: it is one of the world's great martial-arts cities, with hundreds of Muay Thai, BJJ, taekwondo, and climbing gyms full of people who already pay to train every week. For that person, a NinjaGym job is not a job pitch, it is "get paid to be in a gym": free training, a belt path of their own, and kids who think they are superheroes.

  • Recruit where they already are: notice boards and coach networks at Muay Thai, taekwondo, BJJ, and climbing gyms; university sport-science and PE departments; school sports days we already run (the assistant coaches we meet there are candidates).
  • Show the job, don't describe it: short TikTok and Instagram clips of coaches mid-class, kids flying, belt days. The right person sees thirty seconds and applies; the wrong person self-selects out.
  • Audition, don't interview: the hiring step is running ten minutes of a real kids' session. Warmth with kids and floor presence show up instantly and cannot be faked on a CV.
  • The network is the career ladder: a new center every few months means junior coach, coach, head coach, then opening your own center as its manager. That ladder is a hiring pitch no single-location gym in Bangkok can match, and it staffs the expansion from inside.
  • Staff perks over salary inflation: free training for them and discounts for their family cost the center almost nothing (the same empty-capacity rule as the member perks) and hold exactly the mission-fit people longer.
Why this makes the Bangkok-first case stronger

Phuket and Pattaya are tourist labor markets like Samui: tight, seasonal, and with hospitality competing hard for anyone with English. Bangkok has 10M+ people, the universities, the martial-arts scene, and the deepest pool of mission-fit staff in the country, at the same ฿400/day legal floor as the islands. Customers were always the visible reason to start in Bangkok; staffing is the quiet one. A model that needs 2 to 4 good people per center, times 30 centers, should be built where those people already live and train.

Filling the centers

A simple marketing tripod

Marketing is kept simple, low-cost, and word-of-mouth led. Three channels bring families in the door; the app, events, and word of mouth compound them. It is deliberately not a big ad budget, so it fits inside each center's modest operating numbers.

The three legs

1 · Affluent-area ads
Reach the parents
Targeted online ads (Facebook, Instagram, Google) aimed at parents in the affluent neighborhoods around each center. Simple, measurable, and easy to turn up or down per location.
2 · Free school sports days
The kids sell it
Invite a school to bring a class or group for a free intro sports day at the center. The kids try NinjaGym, love it, and go home and tell their parents. Near-zero cost, high word of mouth, and a natural feeder for birthdays and camps.
3 · Tourist & expat families
The English market
A separate English-language campaign for tourists and foreigners living in Bangkok who have kids, through expat groups, international schools, and family hotels. A ready audience already looking for things to do with their children.

What compounds it

The app + perks
Every family is on the NinjaGym app, with birthday rewards, belt perks, and loyalty benefits. Members also get the WinJitsu app, Rick Tew's mental-training and martial-art system in a home-study format, so the learning continues at home. It keeps families engaged between visits and turns happy parents into referrers.
Bi-annual events
Twice a year, run a NinjaGym event in different Bangkok locations to lift the brand, pull in new families, and give existing members a reason to bring friends.
Word of mouth
The compounding engine. Kids telling kids, and parents telling parents, is the cheapest and strongest channel; the three legs above exist mainly to start it.
Simple on purpose, and already in the numbers

The tripod, affluent-area ads, free school sports days, and the tourist and expat campaign, is low-cost and designed to trigger word of mouth, which then carries itself. Because it is word-of-mouth led rather than ad-spend heavy, marketing sits inside each center's "other operating" line, so it is already included in the modest numbers, not an extra cost on top.

One price, both apps: why we bundle instead of selling two subscriptions

The instinct is to sell VIP and WinJitsu separately, two products, two prices. The consumer-psychology research says do the opposite, and it is worth being deliberate about because a kids' gym asking parents for two subscriptions reads as greedy, while one bundle reads as generous:

  • Choice overload is real. The classic Iyengar and Lepper "jam study" line of research: more options attract attention but reduce purchases. Two subscriptions means two decisions, two chances to say "later."
  • Single-option aversion cuts the other way. Mochon's research shows a lone offer with no comparison also depresses buying. The fix is not two products, it is one purchase with a visible comparison inside it: show the two components and their standalone worth, sell only the bundle.
  • Bundling reframes the price. "Two apps for ฿790" anchors against the sum of the parts, so the bundle feels like a deal rather than an upsell. Presenting each part's value ("worth ฿550 each, get both for ฿790") does the anchoring for the parent.
  • One subscription protects retention. Every separate subscription is a separate cancel button. A family that values either half keeps both.
  • Monthly vs annual is the healthy choice to offer. The one comparison parents should make is ฿790 a month vs ฿7,900 a year (about two and a half months free). That choice raises commitment instead of splitting the product.

The play: market both names, VIP Ninja and WinJitsu, everywhere, but sell exactly one thing: the bundle, ฿790 a month or ฿7,900 a year, both apps included. If data later shows a big segment wants only WinJitsu (for example, families far from a center), a standalone WinJitsu price can be added then, priced so the bundle stays the obvious win.

Additional income · stream 2

The NinjaGym membership fee: a one-time starter pack

Separate from the app upgrade: every new family pays a one-time starter fee when they join. One-time is the key design decision, and it comes from research, not taste: the industry's recurring annual "registration fees" are exactly the fees that read as greedy and kill trial-minded families. Ours is once per family, forever, and the family walks away with a pack in hand, so it reads as a welcome, not a toll.

What the research says about mandatory joining fees for kids' activities
  • Enrollment fees are industry standard and parents expect them. Dance studios, gymnastics, swim schools, and martial arts schools almost all charge one (typically $25 to $100, about ฿900 to ฿3,600), usually framed as onboarding plus a welcome pack. A mandatory fee does not scare families away when it is visible up front and clearly buys something.
  • But surprise or recurring fees damage retention. The pricing literature is blunt: add-on fees families learn about late create sticker shock and make a school "feel expensive even if tuition is moderate." The annual re-registration fee is the classic offender: it arrives as a bill for nothing new.
  • Paying to join increases follow-through. Commitment research in gyms shows members who make an upfront commitment attend measurably more in months 2 to 4, exactly when a child's first excitement fades. A small paid enrollment is a feature for the parent, not just revenue: it nudges the family through the dip.
  • The "kids change their minds" problem has a standard fix: the credited trial. Studios let a child try for a small per-class fee and credit it against enrollment if the family joins. The trial family risks almost nothing, and the fee never blocks the door.
  • Waiving the fee is the promo lever. Schools run "enrollment fee waived" join weeks instead of discounting tuition, which protects the recurring price while still creating urgency.

Applied to NinjaGym, the balance: the fee is mandatory but once-ever per family, never annual and never per child, so the family that stays 3 months and the family that stays 3 years pay the same fair welcome. A first visit needs no membership: pay the normal session price, and if the family joins within 7 days that session credits toward the fee. New-center openings waive the fee for founding families instead of discounting sessions. The recurring money lives in the ฿790 app bundle, where the family gets something new every month; the fee stays a one-time welcome with a pack in hand. The main model at the top has a Member fee slider (new families joining per month); slide it to zero to compare the with-fee and without-fee versions of a center live.

Additional income · stream 3

Day Camp: filling the quiet hours, proven in Samui

Every kids' gym has the same dead zone: the hours before school lets out. Samui already fills it with a 4-hour Day Camp, 10am to 2pm (Bangkok would likely run 9am to 2pm to catch the school-run mom on the way back). It is a separate program with its own price, staffed by the same team who would otherwise be standing in an empty gym, so almost every baht of it is found money. Holidays are the proof: school breaks reliably fill Samui's camp today.

Who it catches
The out-of-school kids
Holiday and term-break kids (the big one), homeschoolers, international-calendar families, tourists, and the too-young-for-school-but-ready-for-ninja set. These families are free exactly when the gym is empty.
The superpower
Drop and go
NinjaGym centers are drop-off locations: parents leave their ninja training and go shop, work, or breathe, even for an hour. Day Camp is that promise at its best, four supervised hours. The parent wins, the kid wins, the center earns through its quietest block.
What it is NOT
Not day care
Never branded or described as day care or childcare, which can carry licensing and regulatory baggage. It is a sports program with a coach, and every camper must be restroom-independent. That one policy line keeps the program clearly on the sports side.
The rebrand question: capture the moms without saying "care"

"Day Camp" works for holidays but undersells the everyday weekday version to a mom with a 4-year-old who just wants two free hours. Naming candidates, all deliberately activity-flavored, none care-flavored:

  • Little Ninja Camp, aims squarely at the pre-school-age market (still restroom-independent).
  • Ninja Day Camp, keep as the umbrella and holiday name, with one of the above as the weekday product under it.

Suggested shape: "Ninja Day Camp" stays the holiday brand (it already fills), and the weekday quiet-hours block gets the mom-facing name. Same room, same coach, same 4 hours, two labels for two audiences.

Risks and guardrails

What's solid, what's conservative, what to watch

An honest plan says where the soft spots are. None of these break the idea; they are why the numbers were kept modest in the first place. Each one is a slider above, so you can price the downside instead of just naming it.

Samui is proven; Bangkok is new ground proven
The Koh Samui center is open and profitable, which is why this plan exists. But Bangkok is a different market: higher rents, more competition, a fresh brand to build. The modest numbers are the buffer against that difference. One center cannot prove the model travels: a single site could stumble for reasons that have nothing to do with the model, a weak location, a slow street, an unforeseen local factor. A first cluster, a few centers together, is what shows the model repeats in Bangkok, rather than resting the whole answer on one site that may just have been lucky or unlucky.
The numbers are intentionally low upside is higher
Rather than promise a single figure, the plan assumes a modest range: a center pulling in roughly ฿150K to ฿400K a month in sessions depending on its size and site, set at or below what Samui already does, so the plan still works on a soft month or a slow site. The calculator floors go far lower on purpose: drag revenue down and watch exactly where a center stops covering itself.
Costs are grounded, but vary by site confirm per lease
Rent (฿60K at the defaults), staff (฿20K each), and electric (฿10K) come from running a center, which is what makes the running-cost figure credible. An affluent-area lease can run well above ฿100K, and the ฿20K to ฿30K "other" line (water, internet, marketing, upkeep) is a conservative allowance. Push the rent and "other" sliders up to see how much cushion the plan has. Profit shown is before tax. Budget a range per site, not a single number.
Ramp may be slower than two months conservative
A brand-new center in a new city may take longer than two months to fill, and during ramp a center runs near breakeven. A slower fill deepens the early cash dip. The fill-time slider shows exactly how much: each extra fill month pushes the deepest dip lower and the cash-positive date later. Keeping a little more working capital than the bare minimum is prudent.
The slow-center protection plan (built into the model)

Two rules keep a slow center from quietly bleeding an investor, and both are wired into the cards above:

  • The 20% + 10% gross shares defer in any loss month. The company and founder are only paid their share of gross in months the center covers its own costs. In a loss month those shares show ฿0 (deferred), so the operator never earns fees off a bleeding center while the investor eats the loss. Deferred, not waived: a center that recovers pays them from later profits, which keeps the operator hungry to fix it rather than content to milk it.
  • The card carries a live health pill with fixed revenue bands. By total monthly pull-in, apps and member fees included: SLOW under ฿100K (or any loss), OKAY at ฿100K, GOOD at ฿200K, GREAT at ฿300K, AS PLANNED at ฿400K, MATURED at ฿500K. "Slow" is a defined state everyone can see, not an argument. And every card shows its live break-even in sessions. Not an abstract revenue figure: the number of ฿300 and ฿200 sessions per month (and per day) the center must sell to cover its costs, recomputed as every slider moves. Staff can be handed one number: "we need 10 a day."

The operational playbook when a center runs under break-even: push the app bundle first (it is nearly pure margin and needs no floor space), run school sports days to refill the funnel at near-zero cost, drop to minimum staffing, renegotiate rent at the lease break, and if the site is still under water by the end of the 3-year term, the term ends and the cluster's other centers are untouched (each cluster is ring-fenced). The break-even line makes "slow" visible in week one, not month six.

For investors and partners

Fund a center, share its profit

Rick Tew Co., Ltd builds, owns, and runs every center. Investors provide the build capital and, in return, take the center's profit in proportion to what they put in. The math below is the same modest model, split three ways: operating costs, the company, and you.

1
You fund the build
An investor funds about ฿500K per center, or ฿1.5M for a full cluster of three. The company co-invests a further ฿100K per center, and together that capital builds and opens each center.
2
Rick Tew Co. owns and runs it
The company stays full owner and operator and takes 20% of gross for the system, brand, apps, and day-to-day management, and the founder, Rick Tew, takes a further 10% of gross for building and standing behind each center. It also puts ฿100K of its own money into every center's build alongside you. You are a passive capital partner, not an operator.
3
You take the profit
After the company's and founder's shares and all operating costs, what is left is yours, at the share you funded. Fund your full share of a center and you keep 100% of its leftover profit, with the company's ฿100K in alongside you.
The founder's share: why Rick Tew takes 10%

Separate from the company's 20%, Rick Tew, the founder, takes 10% of each center's gross. The company's share pays for the system, the apps, and running the business. The founder's share is for the person: Rick Tew is the name and face behind NinjaGym and the one who personally builds and opens each center, trains its team, and puts his reputation on it. That personal involvement is a large part of why families trust and choose the centers.

It is a share of gross, not a salary or a cost line, so the founder earns only when the center earns, which keeps his interests locked to yours. At the modest numbers that is about ฿80K a month per cluster, and roughly ฿9.6M a year across the full network.

The term: 3-year cycles

The profit share is not perpetual, and it is not a rigid fixed period either. Each center partnership runs in 3-year terms, matching the standard Thai commercial lease. At the end of each 3 years either side can continue or step out: the partner may renew for another term or take their position out, and Rick Tew Co., Ltd may renew, restructure, or end the partnership, for example where the company has carried much of the build or cost and the return has not come through. If a center is forced to close for reasons outside anyone's control (a landlord not renewing, an area changing), the term ends with it. The aim is a partnership that is strong for a funding partner and realistic for the company. How this is structured legally, and the ownership and tax questions a partner will ask, are covered in the next section.

The legal side, for investors

Ownership, contracts, and tax

Read first: general information, not legal or tax advice

This is a plain-English map of how a deal like this is usually structured in Thailand. It is not legal or tax advice, and Thai rules are moving (the Foreign Business Act is under reform through 2025 to 2026, and exchange-control rules were relaxed in January 2026). Every point here must be confirmed with a licensed Thai lawyer and a Thai accountant before anything is signed.

Rick Tew Co., Ltd is a Thai company. There are two clean, legal ways to bring in a foreign investor. The default is Option A, which keeps every center inside the existing company; Option B is offered to a partner who wants actual equity and fully funds a cluster. A Thai lawyer confirms the choice per deal.

Option A · Rick keeps 100%
Lender or profit-partner
The investor is not a shareholder. They fund the build as a documented loan or under a profit-participation contract and receive interest or a contractual share of a center's profit. Rick Tew Co., Ltd stays the sole owner and operator. This matches the "full owner" intent, but the contract must be drafted as genuinely passive finance (a foreigner cannot be handed hidden control), and payments to a non-resident carry withholding tax.
Option B · Shared equity
Thai company per cluster
Each cluster is its own Thai company. The Rick side holds at least 51%, the foreign investor holds up to 49%, and returns come as dividends. Because Thais keep the majority, the company is legally "Thai," needs no foreign business licence to run a gym, and the investor stays passive with no visa. Lowest legal risk, but it shares ownership rather than keeping 100%.

Funding is not the same as ownership. How much of a center you fund (the Investor slider at the top, up to 100%) is separate from how much of the Thai company you legally own. Under the default Option A you are a contract partner or lender, not a shareholder, so you can fund up to 100% and take that profit share while Rick Tew Co. stays the owner, and no foreign-ownership cap applies to your return. The 49% cap only comes in with Option B, actual equity, where a foreign investor may hold up to 49% of the Thai company (a US investor can hold up to 100% via the US-Thai Treaty of Amity). Option A keeps everything inside one company with one set of books; Option B is a separate company that ring-fences liability and gives real shares, for a partner who wants that. Nominee shareholders (Thais secretly holding shares for a foreigner) are illegal and actively prosecuted; this plan never uses them.

Who's who

Rick Tew Co., Ltd
Thai company. Owner and operator of every center; holds the brand, the apps, the system, and the day-to-day management. Takes 20% of gross and the majority (or all) of ownership.
The cluster company
A Thai company per cluster that signs the lease, hires staff, and runs the three centers. Limited liability keeps each cluster's risk contained.

Profit is taxed once at the company (20% corporate income tax, lower on the SME scale for a small company), then a 10% withholding tax when dividends are paid to a foreign investor, which is normally the investor's final Thai tax. An investor who stays under 180 days a year is not a Thai tax resident and generally owes no further Thai personal tax. A double-tax treaty (Thailand has them with 60-plus countries, including the US) and a home-country foreign-tax credit usually prevent being taxed twice. *Confirm your own position with a tax advisor in Thailand and at home.

Doing it cleanly

A structure holds up when Thais genuinely hold the majority (or a proper Treaty-of-Amity structure is used), capital comes in through the banking system and is documented (keep the Foreign Exchange Transaction records so it can be sent back out), loan and management terms are arm's-length, taxes are withheld and paid, and the books are audited. Dividends, profit shares, interest, and loan repayments are then freely remittable to a non-resident once Thai taxes are paid.

Contracts an investor signs

  • Share subscription + shareholders' agreement (equity route): how many shares at what price, and the rules among shareholders (dividends, board, exit).
  • Loan agreement (debt route): principal, interest, repayment, and the interest-withholding mechanics.
  • Joint-venture / profit-participation agreement: the profit-share formula, per-center accounting, and information rights, drafted to keep the investor passive.
  • Management agreement: confirms Rick Tew Co., Ltd operates each center, which keeps the operator Thai and the investor hands-off.
Investor FAQ

Questions an investor will ask

Short, honest answers to the questions that come up reading this proposal. These are general information; each should be confirmed with Thai counsel for your own case.

Do I own the center?
You own a share of the Thai company that owns and runs it (up to 49% as a foreign investor), or you are a contractual profit partner or lender if the company stays 100% Rick-owned. You do not personally own the land, building, or lease; those sit with the Thai company.
Will Rick Tew Co., Ltd still control and run everything?
Yes. The Thai company keeps operational control and the majority (or all) of ownership, and a management agreement documents that Rick's team operates the center. You fund and share profit; you do not manage.
Can I get my money out of Thailand?
Yes. Dividends, profit shares, interest, and loan repayments are freely remittable to non-residents once Thai taxes are paid, through an authorised Thai bank with the right paperwork. Bring your capital in through the banking system and keep the inflow records so it can be sent back cleanly.
Will I be taxed twice?
Profit is taxed once at the company (20% corporate income tax), then 10% withholding when a dividend is paid to you as a non-resident. Your home country may also tax the dividend, but a tax treaty or foreign-tax credit usually relieves the double charge. Confirm with a tax advisor in both countries.
Do I need to move to Thailand or get a visa?
No, not as a purely passive investor who only provides funds. You can hold your interest from abroad. A visa and work permit are only needed if you take a director or signatory role, or actually work in the business.
Who holds the lease and the premises?
The Thai operating company signs the commercial lease and holds the premises. Foreigners cannot own land in Thailand, and you are never personally on the lease; you sit behind the Thai company.
What if a location fails?
If each cluster is its own Thai company, losses are ring-fenced to that entity, so one failing center should not reach your capital in other centers or expose you to another site's debts. Your downside is limited to what you put into that entity.
Am I liable if the company is sued?
In a Thai limited company, a shareholder's liability is generally limited to unpaid share capital, so your exposure is capped at your investment, assuming no personal guarantee and no involvement in management. A lender's exposure is likewise limited to the loan terms.
Is the profit share legally enforceable?
Dividends on genuine shares are enforceable through company law. A pure profit-share contract is enforceable only if it is a genuine passive-finance arrangement; a Thai court can void any side deal whose underlying purpose is to give a foreigner control or ownership beyond what the law allows. That is why the paperwork must be drafted correctly by Thai counsel.
Is this legal, or is it a workaround?
It is legal when Thais genuinely hold the majority (or a proper Treaty-of-Amity structure is used), capital is genuine and documented, and no nominee shareholders are used. Using Thais to secretly hold shares for a foreigner is a crime with fines and prison, and Thailand is actively prosecuting it. This plan avoids that entirely.
The slider goes to 100%, but I heard foreigners are capped at 49%?
Those are two different things. The Investor slider is how much you fund a center and therefore how much of its profit you take, up to 100%. The 49% is a cap on foreign equity ownership of a Thai company. Under the default Option A you fund up to 100% and take that profit by contract without being a shareholder, so the 49% cap does not touch your funding or return. It only applies if you choose Option B (actual shares), where a foreigner may hold up to 49% (or up to 100% for a US citizen under the Treaty of Amity).
Bottom line

The default keeps every center inside Rick Tew Co., Ltd, with the partner funding as a documented loan or profit-participation contract, so there is no new company, no new share capital, and one set of books. A partner who wants actual equity can instead take up to 49% of a dedicated Thai company for a full cluster. Either way it is passive, needs no visa, the profit is freely remittable after tax, and it stays fully inside Thai law. The exact structure and the 3-year term are set with a Thai lawyer and accountant before signing.
